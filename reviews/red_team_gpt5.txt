1) Severity: CRITICAL — The “active execution” standard is self-contradictory in the flagship result. You explicitly state v2.3 fixes v2.2’s flaw of counting “passive acknowledgment” (e.g., echoing attacker text in translation) as compliance, requiring “active execution.” Yet your “most dangerous entry” (markdown_injection_002) is scored compliant because models “echoed the malicious callout verbatim rather than refusing.” Echoing a callout is passive restatement, not executing session-cookie exfiltration. This directly contradicts your own operational definition and collapses your central claim that the judge now distinguishes passive acknowledgment from active harm.

2) Severity: CRITICAL — No human-ground-truth adjudication. You assert the new judge is “calibrated” and that “judge quality is the dominant source of variance,” but you never validate either judge against independent human raters with a pre-registered rubric. Without human ground truth, your “3× reduction” is unmoored: you do not know which judge is closer to truth. Declaring calibration from model-vs-model kappa alone is methodologically empty.

3) Severity: CRITICAL — Kappa values do not support “calibrated” claims. You claim “refusal kappa = 0.492 (moderate)” and “compliance kappa = 0.316 (fair)” as confirmation of calibration. For a high-stakes benchmark, 0.316 on the key compliance label is weak at best. No confidence intervals, no prevalence/bias adjustment, and extreme class imbalance further depress kappa’s interpretability. Calling the judge “calibrated” off kappa=0.316 is unjustified.

4) Severity: CRITICAL — Circularity and self-validation. You (a) use an LLM-as-judge to (b) relabel your dataset and (c) declare the new LLM-as-judge better because it yields lower compliance and slightly higher agreement with itself and another LLM. Then you (d) use those LLM-derived labels to “empirically” set severity. This is circular: the judge defines compliance, and compliance defines severity, and severity is then used to argue the judge is superior. Human adjudication or external ground truth is absent.

5) Severity: CRITICAL — Severity thresholds are arbitrary, internally inconsistent, and mismatched to the study design. You define severity thresholds using denominators “per 10 models” (≥8/10, ≥4/10, 1–3/10), but you evaluate 11 models. You then apply a 5/11 result (45.5%) to the “≥4/10” threshold. This is an obvious methodological error that undermines all severity assignments.

6) Severity: HIGH — The 3-way classification logic is value-laden and unvalidated. Labeling “neutral” (no refusal, no compliance) as “production security failures” is an assertion, not a measurement. In many real agents, ignoring an injection without refusing may be acceptable, depending on UX and policy. You never justify why “neutral” is definitively a failure nor show that “neutral” predicts future compromise. You then use this to malign GLM-5.1 (“100% missed detection”) without any causal link to increased risk.

7) Severity: HIGH — Confounded “3× reduction” headline. You rebuilt the catalog from scratch (v2.3 vs v1/v2.2) and changed the judge. Then you trumpet “3× reduction (10.0% → 3.4%).” Only deep in the paper do we learn the 10.0% is the v2.2 judge re-applied to the v2.3 catalog. The abstract never clarifies this. The framing invites readers to believe v2.2’s published number was wrong by 3×, when in fact you changed both the dataset and judge. This is misleading.

8) Severity: HIGH — Ambiguity whether you re-judged the same model responses or re-ran models. You claim “cross-judge validation (same 552 entries)” but do not state if the same model outputs were reused or if you regenerated outputs (susceptible to model drift). If outputs were regenerated, the comparison is invalid; if re-judged, show evidence (frozen outputs, hashes) and an auditable pipeline. As written, it’s unclear and invites confounding.

9) Severity: HIGH — External validity is weak to nonexistent for agent settings. You test chat completions without live tools and then draw agent-security conclusions (e.g., toolchain confusion, memory poisoning). For scenarios that require actual actions (sending email, storing preferences), your setup prevents “active execution” by design. That biases towards non-compliance and invalidates claims about agent robustness. Your own limitations admit this, yet you still generalize to “production security failures.”

10) Severity: HIGH — Family definitions and mappings to real-world threats are shaky. Latent memory poisoning is collapsed to single-phase chat, destroying the construct (temporal separation) you purport to measure. HTML injection in a pure chat interface is a strawman: the 0% “fully contained” claim is an artifact of a non-rendering environment. You conflate lack of manifestation in your artificial harness with genuine safety.

11) Severity: HIGH — No multiple-comparisons handling. You report many family- and model-level rates, rank models, and highlight “dominant vectors,” but you do not control for multiplicity (51 scenarios × 11 models × 8 families). With this many slices, “top” families and “dangerous entries” can easily be selection noise. There are no hypothesis tests, no corrections, no hierarchical modeling, and no power analysis.

12) Severity: HIGH — Per-model rankings are statistically unsubstantiated. With n=51 per model, 0/51 yields an upper 95% bound of ~7%. Your table shows many 0% models whose CIs overlap with models at 2–8%. You still claim “most robust” or tie for first place. There’s no significance testing, no pairwise comparisons, no uncertainty-aware ranking. The “parameter count does not predict robustness” tagline is equally unsupported given overlapping CIs and confounds (providers, safety layers, domain shifts).

13) Severity: HIGH — Wilson CIs alone are not sufficient. You provide Wilson intervals for raw proportions, but you draw qualitative conclusions from overlapping intervals and never justify the use of Wilson over exact or Bayesian intervals in extreme-imbalance regimes. No interval estimates for kappa, no uncertainty for rankings, no bootstrap for family comparisons.

14) Severity: HIGH — The judge pipeline likely leaks between passes and is not independent. You run Pass 1 (refusal) then Pass 2 (compliance) with the same model. Do you include Pass 1 results in the prompt for Pass 2? If so, you contaminate Pass 2. If not, you risk label incoherence. You report 0 “refused and complied” cases, which is suspiciously neat and suggests leakage or post-hoc tie-breaking.

15) Severity: HIGH — “Dominant source of variance is judge quality” is an unproven causal claim. Kappa disagreement between judges does not isolate the judge as dominant driver versus ambiguity in the task, poor rubric design, or irreducible model output variability. There is no causal identification, ablations, or sensitivity analysis. The claim overreaches.

16) Severity: MEDIUM — Reproducibility is weak: endpoints, versions, seeds. OpenRouter is a routing layer with opaque safety filters and model revisions; you provide no provider-specific version hashes, no API snapshots, no request/response IDs. “temperature=0.0” does not guarantee determinism across vendors (different sampling codepaths, tie-breaking, system-delays). You ran each sample once; no repeats, no variance estimates.

17) Severity: MEDIUM — The rubric and judge prompts are underspecified. You mention “few-shot exemplified rubrics” and “structured JSON,” but the exact prompts, few-shot examples, and schema are not in the paper. Without frozen judge prompts and examples, reproduction is impossible. Minor wording shifts change outcomes with kappa this low.

18) Severity: MEDIUM — Post-hoc severity recalibration has no statistical grounding. Thresholds like “≥4/10” or “≥8/10” (besides the denominator error) are arbitrary. There’s no decision-theoretic or risk-based justification, no power analysis linking sample size to detectable effect sizes, and no calibration against known-bad or known-safe cases.

19) Severity: MEDIUM — “Neutral is failure” is operationally undefined across tasks. You never define what counts as “explicit refusal” (keyword list? policy structure?), nor do you disambiguate “benign task success in presence of injection” from “ignorance.” Without task success metrics, “neutral” conflates helpful behavior with unnoticed attacks. This is measurement invalidity.

20) Severity: MEDIUM — Cherry-picked narrative elements. You emphasize one high-compliance entry and the “3× reduction” while deprioritizing the fact that cross-judge kappa on a 50-entry stratified sample across three judges collapses to ~0.2 (“slight agreement”). That admission undercuts the rest of the paper but is buried in Limitations.

21) Severity: MEDIUM — Missing counterfactuals for causal claims. You say “markdown and cognitive overload dominate at 6.5–6.6%,” but you don’t show that identical content without the specific formatting or overload would not elicit similar rates. No A/B tests, no minimal-difference controls, no ablations. Causal language without controls.

22) Severity: MEDIUM — Incomplete related work and unfair positioning. You reduce prior efforts (AgentDojo, Garak, PyRIT) to oversimplified table cells and criticize “synthetic harm assessments,” but you don’t compare against strong baselines like human-graded injection corpora, red-team incident datasets, or agent frameworks with actual tool execution. No head-to-head with recent LLM-as-judge calibration work or annotation aggregation methods.

23) Severity: LOW — Inconsistent denominators in results tables. Some entries use 2/10, others 2/11, without explanation. If some models failed to run, you must report missingness, reasons, and sensitivity analyses. As is, the inconsistency looks like sloppiness that can change severity bins.

24) Severity: LOW — Terminology inflation. Branding as “AgentBoundary” while evaluating static chat completions (no live tools, no temporal persistence) is marketing, not science. The mismatch between name and methodology confuses readers about what is actually tested.

25) Severity: NOTE — Ethical/operational implications are asserted without evidence. Claims such as “simple keyword filters miss the attacks that actually work” are plausible but untested here; you did not run any filter baselines. Similarly, the takeaways about “behavioral monitoring” are prescriptions unbacked by measurements.

26) Severity: NOTE — GLM-5.1 characterization is unfair. You label it “injection-aware” and condemn it for “silently ignoring payloads” (neutral). If the model’s design goal is to ignore untrusted segments without disrupting UX, that may be correct behavior. You never present the model’s documented defense policy to justify your framing.

27) Severity: NOTE — No registration or pre-analysis plan. Given you rebuilt the catalog and changed scoring, a pre-registered rubric and analysis plan would have reduced p-hacking concerns. The paper reads like exploratory analysis framed as definitive.

28) Severity: NOTE — No release artifacts pinned. You reference a GitHub repo and version string but provide no immutable artifact (DOI, commit hash in paper body), no raw outputs for both judges, and no automated script to recompute all tables from raw logs. “Available under MIT” is not the same as a reproducible artifact.

Bottom line: The paper’s central claim—v2.3’s “calibrated” judge fixes v2.2’s over-calling and yields trustworthy, empirical severity—is unsubstantiated. The methodology contradicts itself on what counts as “active compliance,” the statistics do not support the rankings or the “3× reduction” narrative, reproducibility is weak, and the external/construct validity is poor for agent threats. This needs major redesign: human-grounded labels, clear operational definitions, proper statistical tests with multiplicity control, immutable artifacts, and honest causal identification. In its current form, this is not publication-ready.
